Drawn to scale for a game: a door is 2.1 m tall and a chair seat 0.45 m high; the floor is flat and even, with clear walkways at least 1 m wide from the entrance to every seat, bed and counter; nothing stands along the open front edge of the floor; every seat faces into the room and is fully visible, never hidden behind other furniture.
  • View. The town sprites' camera: isometric 2:1 (30°) from the south-west. The room is cut away like a dollhouse: the floor and the two far walls (north and east) are drawn, the near walls and the ceiling are left out, and inside walls stop at waist height. That is the view a character will be walked around in, so it must be the same in every picture.
  • To scale, with room to walk. A door is 2.1 m tall, a chair seat 0.45 m high, a bed 2 m long. Each prompt gives the room's size in metres. The floor is flat and even, the walkways are at least 1 m wide, and nothing stands along the open front edge of the floor, so the game can draw people on the floor in front of the furniture.
  • Every seat in view. Chairs, sofas, stools, beds and pews face into the room and are never hidden behind other furniture: each one is a place a character will sit or lie down.
  • A spot for every activity. Each prompt lists what a character can do in that place (from the game's data) and the piece of the room it happens at. Those pieces must be in the picture.
  • Light. Golden hour through the windows (warm, low sun from the west) and warm lamps; the same light in every interior, like the town.
  • Palette (art/STYLE.md). Ivory and sand plaster (#f7ecd6, #f0dec2, #d9c9a9), warm oak, champagne gold for fittings and trim (#dabb85), deep emerald for upholstery, doors and accents (#0b2b23), sage-green plants (#939c87). Black marble and gold (the law-office reference) only where a prompt names it. Exposed brick only where the description says so.
  • Windows. Only sky, trees and what the place's own description names (cranes, the river, the lake). Never a skyline: the founding town has no towers but the ones on the map.
  • Empty and wordless. No people, no pets, no animals except where the place is about them (the shelter's kennels stay empty too), no text except a sign the prompt names. Screens, menu boards and posters are blank or illegible.
  • Transparent background outside the room, PNG, at the size given.

What these prompts are not

  • Walking around inside and sitting are not in the game yet. Today a character who enters a place is inside it, without a position in a room. These pictures are made first so the rooms can be built from them (see "How a picture becomes a room you can walk in").
  • Room sizes and furnishings beyond the description are art direction, written in this script, not facts from the game. The facts under each prompt say which is which.
  • Pictures show no people. Who works or lives somewhere is listed as a fact, so the room suits them, but they are drawn by the game, not into the picture.
  • When a generated picture gets a fact wrong, say so in art/manifest.json under notes, as the other art does. Nobody should mistake a picture for the game's state.

How a picture becomes a room you can walk in

  1. The picture (this pack): one cutaway room at the town's camera angle, to scale, with a piece of furniture for every activity and clear floor between them.
  2. The room's data (planned: data/interiors/<id>.json, checked by ContentDatabase like the rest of the content): the walkable floor traced from the picture, and its spots, each a seat, bed, counter, stage or fixture with its position, the way a character faces there, and the activity it serves (nap → the sofa, shower → the shower, a shop's offers → its counter).
  3. The rules (planned, in World, like every other change of state): moving to a point inside, taking a spot and leaving it. The server decides who sits where, so two people can't take the same chair, and an activity is done at its spot. Clients then draw the room from the picture and each person at their place in it.
