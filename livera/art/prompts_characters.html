Style sheet (already inside every prompt) 
LIVÉRA character sprite sheet for a 2.5D game. The same camera as the Livéra town sprites: isometric 2:1 (30°) view from the south-west, looking down at about 30°, so the person is seen from slightly above, exactly like the buildings they walk past. Warm golden-hour light from the west, the town's light: a soft champagne glow on skin and clothes, a warm rim light, soft shading; clean photoreal render with the realism of the reference photo, no outlines. Transparent background (PNG with alpha): no floor, no ground shadow, no scenery, no props except what the person carries below and the chair and the sofa, no text, no labels, no frame or grid lines. One person, the same in every pose: the same face, hair, skin tone, body, outfit and accessories, at the same scale in every cell, full body from the top of the head to the soles, nothing cropped. No logos or recognisable brands on clothes or shoes.
  • View. The town sprites' camera: isometric 2:1 (30°) from the south-west, looking down at about 30°. People are seen from slightly above, as the buildings are, so they stand in the town instead of on it. The reference sheets are photographs from eye level; the sprites keep the person and change the camera.
  • Light. Golden hour from the west, the same as every town sprite and interior: a soft champagne glow, a warm rim light, soft shading. The game darkens it for the night itself.
  • One sheet per person, 4 × 3 cells. Row 1: a 4-frame walk toward the lower left (three-quarter front). Row 2: a 4-frame walk away toward the upper right (three-quarter back). Row 3: standing idle front and back, sitting on a chair, sitting on a sofa. The other two diagonals are the first two rows mirrored, which covers every street on the map (they all run at 30° on screen).
  • One scale for everyone. A 175 cm person stands 300 px tall in a 341 px cell; each prompt gives that person's height in pixels from their height in the data. Full body, nothing cropped, feet on one baseline per row.
  • The same person in every cell. Face, hair, skin tone, build, outfit and jewellery as in the data and the reference sheet; attach the sheet to the generator with the prompt.
  • Transparent background, PNG, nothing but the person (and the chair and sofa in row 3). No ground shadow: the game draws its own under the feet.
  • No brands. Plain shoes and watches. Where a reference sheet shows something that resembles a real product, the prompt says to draw it plain.

What these prompts are not

  • The game draws the filed sheets' frames (D-072: scripts/client_figures.py cuts them, web/client/figures.js draws them) on the map and in the rooms. People without a sheet are still drawn from their model sheet's cut-outs: one photograph per direction, scaled, with a bob for walking and a standing figure lowered into the seat for sitting.
  • The outfit is the data's. Where a reference sheet shows another look (Dominique's turnaround is her weekend look, Dax's an all-black tee), the prompt asks for the outfit in the data; the sheet is for the face, hair, skin and build.
  • Everyone's look comes from their data (data/characters/<id>.json, profile.appearance). The four residents (Kai Morgan, Rosa Delgado, Sol Rivera, Theo Grant) were made in the creator for the town-preview recording and are data characters since D-073; their facts were read off their sheets.
  • The townspeople have no picture yet. Each has two prompts: a model sheet first (the same layout as the cast's: portrait, name card, two looks, four expressions, a four-view turnaround), then the sprite sheet, made with that model sheet attached. Until their art is filed the game draws them as a labelled stand-in in their work colours (web/client/figures.js).
  • When a generated picture gets a fact wrong, say so (art_notes in their data, or notes in art/manifest.json), as the other art does. Nobody should mistake a picture for the game's state.

How a sheet replaces the cut-outs

  1. The sheet (this pack): art/sprites/characters/<id>/sheet.png, 4 × 3 cells as above (filed as sheet.webp).
  2. The frames (scripts/client_figures.py cuts the cells): walk_front34_1 to _4, walk_back34_1 to _4, idle_front34, idle_back34, sit_chair, sit_sofa, each cut out with the repository's BiRefNet matte, scaled like the cut-outs to 360 px for a 1.75 m person, anchored at the feet (sitting: the seat point, where the seat is under the hips), into web/client/figures/<id>/ and its manifest. The chair and the sofa drawn in row 3 are taken out of the seated frames (the room's own seat is under the person), so draw them as asked: a simple cream chair on oak legs, a green velvet sofa.
  3. The client (web/client/figures.js, same drawFigure() API): the walk frames for pose: "walk" by phase, the standing frames for standing, the seated frames for pose: "sit" on the seat (chair, stool and seat: sit_chair; sofa and bed: sit_sofa); mirrored for the other two diagonals. A seat that faces away from the camera still shows the standing back frame lowered onto it: the sheet has no seated frame from behind. Fighting (fists up) and flinching are not in these sheets: today pose: "fight" and "flinch" lean, crouch and bounce or shake the standing picture. A later sheet can ask for them, and for sitting seen from behind.
